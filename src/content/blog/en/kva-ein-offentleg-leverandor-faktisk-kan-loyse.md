## Eight days between diagnosis and budget

Eight days passed between the government pointing to the problem and presenting the bill. The bill shows why the problem persists.

On 29 September 2026, Norway's Ministry of Digitalisation and Public Governance announced a new white paper to parliament (*stortingsmelding*) [\[1\]](#ref-1). The white paper will address digital sovereignty and value creation. The reasoning in the press release is direct. Norway depends heavily on digital services from a handful of large companies outside Europe. Geopolitical instability makes it necessary to assess what this dependency means for security, preparedness, and national room for manoeuvre [\[1\]](#ref-1).

Eight days later, on 7 October 2026, the government presented its proposed state budget for 2027 [\[2\]](#ref-2). In it, the government proposes 2,151.2 million kroner (around 200 million US dollars) for new digitalisation measures. That is up from roughly 1.6 billion kroner the year before [\[2\]](#ref-2)[\[3\]](#ref-3). Of this sum, 121.3 million kroner goes to six dedicated artificial intelligence initiatives [\[3\]](#ref-3).

When you read a budget alongside an announced white paper, it is easy to be dazzled by the headline figure. Two billion kroner sounds like an offensive. The real question is what the money actually buys. Equally important is who sits at the other end of the transaction once the money is spent.

The chronology behind the budget does not start in September. On 12 September 2025, the Norwegian Agency for Public and Financial Management (DFØ) signed the largest public cloud contracts in Norwegian history [\[4\]](#ref-4). They were signed through the Cloud Services Marketplace (*Markedsplassen for skytjenester*). The four parallel framework agreements cover general cloud-based infrastructure and platform services, known as CIPS. Their combined ceiling is up to ten billion kroner over four years. The contracts went to Amazon Web Services, Google Cloud, IBM, and Oracle [\[4\]](#ref-4).

The consultancy firms Menon Economics and A-2 Norge previously mapped the public cloud market for DFØ. They found that Microsoft holds between 40 and 60 per cent of the public-sector market in Norway [\[5\]](#ref-5). Microsoft stood outside this specific joint agreement, yet it still dominates through separate contracts for office software and platforms [\[5\]](#ref-5).

On 11 February 2026, six members of parliament from the Socialist Left Party (SV) tabled Document 8:119 S on digital sovereignty in turbulent times [\[6\]](#ref-6). They called for a national cloud service owned and operated by the public sector. They also asked for a plan to phase out the major international platforms in central government administration. The Standing Committee on Local Government and Public Administration delivered its recommendation, Innst. 225 S, on 28 April. The Storting, Norway's parliament, debated the motion on 5 May. On 7 May 2026, a majority comprising the Labour Party, the Centre Party, and the Conservative Party voted the proposal down [\[7\]](#ref-7)[\[8\]](#ref-8).

In August 2026, the government and the National Library of Norway launched the Borealis family of language models [\[9\]](#ref-9). The models were trained on Olivia, the state-owned supercomputer in Nordfjordeid in western Norway. As I showed in part two of this series, the model was uploaded as a download on Hugging Face [\[10\]](#ref-10). Nobody was commissioned to run it as a live service for the public sector [\[10\]](#ref-10).

Then came October and the 2027 state budget. There stands the verdict on what the state actually chooses to do.

Before I examine the budget lines, my own ties belong in the text. I work as a senior adviser at Lånekassen, the Norwegian State Educational Loan Fund. It is a state agency that buys and operates digital services itself. I am a member of the Socialist Left Party (SV), which tabled the parliamentary motion in May. I am also a member of the union NITO, the Norwegian Society of Engineers and Technologists, which has demanded stronger national control over digital infrastructure. I write here in a personal capacity. The language model I use as a tool while writing is developed by Google. Google is one of the four suppliers on DFØ's ten-billion-kroner framework agreement, and it owns the Gemma licence on which Borealis is built.

## Five million against the token bill

The table of new digitalisation measures in the 2027 budget proposition, Prop. 1 S (2026–2027), counts seventeen lines [\[2\]](#ref-2). The major items are familiar tasks in public administration. The police receive 500 million kroner for comprehensive digitalisation. Telecom security and emergency preparedness receive 451.3 million. Nav, the Norwegian Labour and Welfare Administration, receives 332.6 million to modernise benefit payments. The Norwegian Digitalisation Agency (Digdir) receives 105.7 million for shared digital solutions [\[2\]](#ref-2).

The line that tells us most about the direction, however, is the 121.3 million kroner for new AI initiatives [\[2\]](#ref-2)[\[3\]](#ref-3). The money is split across four ministries and six measures:

First, AI Norway (*KI Norge*) under Digdir receives an extra 30 million kroner. The unit received 30 million when it was established in September 2025. Now its mandate is expanded to cover AI safety, risk assessment, and evaluation of language models [\[3\]](#ref-3)[\[11\]](#ref-11).

Second, Digdir receives 25 million kroner to establish a shared solution for generative AI in the public sector [\[3\]](#ref-3).

Third, the Ministries' Digitalisation Organisation (DIO) and the Office of the Prime Minister receive 21.3 million kroner. The funding goes to AI and digitalisation inside the ministries [\[3\]](#ref-3).

Fourth, the Norwegian Water Resources and Energy Directorate (NVE) receives 20 million kroner. The agency will use generative AI to process licensing applications for power generation and grid construction [\[3\]](#ref-3).

Fifth, 20 million kroner goes to a public AI service for health advice on Helsenorge, the national health portal [\[3\]](#ref-3).

Sixth, 5 million kroner goes to DFØ to strengthen the Cloud Services Marketplace [\[3\]](#ref-3).

This sixth item deserves to be read with a magnifying glass. Five million kroner is pocket change in a national budget. Yet the justification in the budget proposal is revealing. It shows how the cost structure of public-sector IT is shifting.

The allocation to DFØ is justified by a sharp rise in the cost of cloud and AI services [\[3\]](#ref-3). The major AI companies are moving to token-based pricing, and graphics processing units (GPUs) are expensive to run [\[3\]](#ref-3). The measure aims to increase the state's collective bargaining power, meet needs across the public sector, and secure more favourable prices [\[3\]](#ref-3).

Read those two statements side by side. On 29 September, the government says that dependence on a few foreign technology companies is a vulnerability [\[1\]](#ref-1). On 7 October, the government observes that those very same companies are changing their pricing model. They are moving from fixed licences to ongoing payment per piece of text, known as a token. The bill is growing. The budget's answer is to allocate five million kroner to the procurement officers at DFØ, so they can negotiate a discount on the token price.

At the same time, there is a negative finding in the budget. What is missing is as instructive as what is included. The state allocates 2,151.2 million kroner to new digitalisation measures and 121.3 million to AI. Yet not a single krone goes to running the National Library's Borealis model in production as a shared service [\[2\]](#ref-2)[\[3\]](#ref-3). The state already owns the hardware through Sigma2, the national research computing body. The state also pays 45 million kroner a year to Kopinor, the Norwegian reproduction rights organisation, for the training data [\[9\]](#ref-9). Yet when public agencies connect to generative AI in 2027, the state budgets for haggling with commercial cloud providers.

## The middleware that does not own the floor

What about the 25 million kroner to Digdir for a shared generative AI solution? The ministry writes that the solution will act as a common foundation across public agencies [\[3\]](#ref-3). It will handle requirements for security, privacy, logging, access to relevant data, and connection to language models. The goal is to spare every individual agency from building this on its own [\[3\]](#ref-3).

This is sensible engineering, and it solves a real problem. Today, municipalities, directorates, and hospital trusts sit separately with the exact same tasks. They write the same data protection impact assessments. They build the same wrappers around the same application programming interfaces (APIs). They each pay their own consultants to set up the same access controls. A shared wrapper at Digdir saves duplicated effort.

Yet a wrapper is not an engine. The phrase in the budget proposal is "connection to language models" [\[3\]](#ref-3). Digdir's shared solution is priced at 25 million kroner. It is a middleware layer. The service is a shared gateway that receives the prompt from the caseworker. It scrubs and logs the request according to Norwegian rules, and forwards it to a language model located somewhere else.

Twenty-five million kroner is enough to pay around twenty developers and lawyers for a year. It does not buy a single stack of high-bandwidth memory (HBM) or a single GPU cluster. The inference itself, the computation that produces the answer, still has to run on a machine that somebody owns. Nobody has been given a mandate or a budget to run the Norwegian models as a national shared service on Sigma2. Consequently, the cable from Digdir's middleware runs straight out into the commercial cloud contracts managed by DFØ.

This is not a criticism of the civil servants at Digdir or DFØ. They are carrying out the assignments they were given. The boundaries were set when the parliamentary majority voted down a public cloud service in May [\[7\]](#ref-7). The flaw lies in the core premise of Norwegian digitalisation policy. Policymakers believe they can regulate and negotiate their way to sovereignty at the top of the stack while renting the bottom.

To see why that equation does not add up, we have to split the problem into three. The digital economy has three distinct class divides. In public debate, they are almost always blurred into one. The three are **access**, **quality**, and **ownership of returns**.

A public provider, or a joint public procurement scheme, can solve the first. It can partly cushion the second. It does not touch the third, unless the public sector actually owns the means of production itself.

## The first divide: Access

The first class divide is about who can afford and manage to use the tools at all.

In the consumer market, this divide is easy to spot. A subscription to the top language models costs twenty or two hundred dollars a month per user. For a knowledge worker in Oslo, that is trivial. For a student or a household on a tight budget, it is an expense that counts. In the public sector, the exact same divide runs between agencies. On one side stand large, well-resourced bodies like the Tax Administration and Nav. On the other stands a small municipality with three thousand residents.

Menon Economics and A-2 Norge documented this in their report for DFØ [\[5\]](#ref-5). Between 60 and 70 per cent of public-sector bodies lacked a clear cloud strategy, or were unsure whether they had one [\[5\]](#ref-5). Small municipalities do not have procurement lawyers to negotiate with global technology firms. Nor do they have security architects to audit the terms of service.

This is the problem that Digdir's 25-million-kroner shared solution and DFØ's 5-million-kroner marketplace actually solve [\[3\]](#ref-3). The state creates a joint contract and a shared technical framework. The small municipality then gains access to the same toolbox and the same legal safeguards as the large directorate. In the same way, a public provider offering a free language model to schools and citizens would level out access. Nobody should be left without tools because the municipal treasury is empty.

This is a genuine question of distribution. It belongs in welfare policy. Equal access to digital tools resembles the public library or rural broadband roll-out. The measure raises the floor so that nobody falls completely behind.

Yet access is a consumer good. Everyone may be allowed into the shop at the same price. That still does not change who owns the shop. Nor does it change what goods sit in the back room, or where the revenue travels when the till is cashed up at night. When politicians describe joint procurement schemes as digital sovereignty, they confuse a discount card with ownership.

## The second divide: Quality and rationing

The second class divide does not run between those on the inside and those left outside. It runs between those who get first-tier hardware and frontier models, and those who get the leftover capacity.

In part three of this series, I showed how this works in the hardware layer [\[12\]](#ref-12). The three memory manufacturers Samsung, SK hynix, and Micron control 87 per cent of the global DRAM market. They have tied up their HBM capacity in multi-year contracts with the major US cloud providers [\[12\]](#ref-12). The rest of the market does not compete in the same auction. It gets whatever is left over once the hyperscalers have taken their share.

The exact same rationing mechanism operates in the software and cloud market. Norway's public sector has already hit this wall twice in 2026.

The first example is the Norwegian Tax Administration (*Skatteetaten*). As the technology publication digi.no reported, the agency wanted to adopt the newest AI tools on Microsoft Azure [\[13\]](#ref-13). However, Microsoft and Databricks did not prioritise the Norwegian data centre region for the latest features. The agency therefore had to consider moving IT workloads out of Norway to foreign data centres just to get access [\[13\]](#ref-13). Norway had a contract. Norway was paying. Yet in the global queue for new hardware and new models, a small, peripheral region stands at the back of the line.

The second example came in June 2026. The engineering unions NITO and Tekna, together with the Norwegian Consumer Council, pointed out that US authorities had blocked foreign access to the most powerful AI tools from Anthropic [\[14\]](#ref-14). The justification was US national security interests [\[14\]](#ref-14).

Here we see what a public provider can and cannot do about the quality divide. If Norway puts Borealis into full production on Olivia and the European supercomputer LUMI-AI, civil servants get a safe, domestically developed alternative. Nobody in Washington or Seattle can switch it off. For summarising case documents, translating into Nynorsk and Sámi, and looking up Norwegian statutes, that quality is more than good enough.

For heavier reasoning, complex code analysis, and specialised scientific tasks, the situation is different. There is still a gap between a model with 26 billion parameters and the largest frontier models. The biggest corporations spend tens of billions of dollars a year on compute. A basic public service will not close that gap on its own. It makes us less vulnerable to coercion in everyday administration. Even so, the base model underneath Borealis is still rented from Google under the Gemma licence [\[10\]](#ref-10). Thus even the Norwegian quality alternative remains bound to how much technology the landlord chooses to share.

## The third divide: Ownership of returns

The third class divide is the structurally decisive one. Even so, it disappears completely in the debate over shared solutions and purchasing power.

In a classic software market, an organisation bought a licence for a tool. It installed the program on its own servers and let its staff work with it. If the employees found a smarter way of working that doubled their productivity, the entire gain stayed with the organisation. The vendor had been paid for the software. The work performed with it belonged to the buyer.

The shift to cloud-based AI services with token pricing breaks this contract.

Look at the allocation of 20 million kroner to the energy regulator NVE in the 2027 state budget [\[3\]](#ref-3). The money is earmarked for generative AI in processing licensing applications for power plants and grid lines. The goal is less manual labour and faster case processing [\[3\]](#ref-3). Everyone can support that goal. The queue of power and grid projects is long, and NVE sits right in the bottleneck.

Now follow the money and the data through the process. A caseworker at NVE uses a rented language model through a cloud contract. Every consultation response, every environmental impact assessment, and every draft decision is sent through the cloud provider's meter. The price is no longer paid for the right to keep the program on the machine. The price is paid per word read and written, per token.

Two consequences follow from this model.

First, the bill scales with usage. NVE, Nav, the police, and the ministries are redesigning casework around AI agents and automated workflows. The more they automate, the more tokens pass through the mill. The state hopes to reap an efficiency gain in saved working hours. Yet that gain meets a variable levy that grows in lockstep with production. That is why DFØ already has to ask for extra funding in the 2027 budget to cope with token-based pricing [\[3\]](#ref-3).

Second, the capital and the learning remain on the wrong side of the contract. Thousands of Norwegian caseworkers, lawyers, and engineers spend their working days on a proprietary cloud platform. They craft prompts, correct errors, and build workflows. In doing so, they build institutional dependency. Public-sector work processes are moulded around the supplier's interfaces. The return on the infrastructure, what economists call economic rent, accrues to whoever owns the platform. It does not accrue to whoever performs the work.

Economists such as Yanis Varoufakis have called this technofeudalism. Profit earned by selling a commodity in a market is replaced by rent. That rent is extracted for access to an enclosed digital commons.

Here lies the paradox in the 2027 budget that ties the whole chain together. What will NVE use its 20 million AI kroner to license? Among other things, power generation and grid connections. And who stands at the front of the queue for Norwegian electricity and grid capacity, according to the government's own press release of 29 September? The data centre industry [\[1\]](#ref-1).

Norwegian public administration is thus renting token capacity from international cloud corporations to process power and grid applications for data centres. The state demands no equity stake in the compute capacity. Nor does the state use its 2.2 billion digitalisation kroner to run its own language model on its own machines. The state provides the hydropower, the land, the road network, and the AI-accelerated permitting process. Then the state pays rent per token to use what comes out at the other end.

This third divide concerns ownership of the returns and of the means of production themselves. No public procurement portal can solve it. A procurement portal is by definition a tenant who has teamed up with other tenants. Together they ask the landlord for a five per cent discount on the rent. They still do not become owners of the building.

## The counterarguments in their strongest form

There are weighty professional objections to the state owning and operating more of this chain itself. They deserve to be tested in their strongest form.

**Digdir's shared solution gives us the freedom to switch models.** This is the strongest technical counterargument, and it has real merit. Digdir can build a clean abstraction layer between the agencies' casework systems and the language models at the back. Then Nav and NVE will not have to bind their code directly to OpenAI, Anthropic, or Google. The state can swap out the model behind the curtain whenever a better or cheaper one appears. It can also plug in Borealis later.

My answer is that an abstraction layer lowers the cost of switching between rental offers. It still does not create an owned alternative to switch *to*. A wall socket makes it easy to switch electricity suppliers, but the socket does not generate electricity. No operating funds are included to keep a public inference service running on Sigma2 and LUMI-AI. Consequently, the cables behind Digdir's shared solution will still only run to the commercial cloud vendors. We will have made the tenancy tidier, not ended it.

**The state should deliver welfare, not tie up billions in hardware that becomes obsolete in two years.** This is the classic economic argument. It carried the majority when Document 8:119 S was voted down in parliament in May 2026 [\[7\]](#ref-7). Graphics processors age at a pace that public budget cycles are not built to handle. When you rent per token through DFØ's contracts, the vendor bears the depreciation risk. The state pays only for what it actually uses.

This argument would have been decisive if the state had not *already* bought the hardware and the models. Yet Norway has already bought Olivia with 448 GH200 superchips through Sigma2. Norway committed 20.4 million euros to LUMI-AI on 1 September 2026. And Norway already pays 45 million kroner a year to train Borealis [\[9\]](#ref-9)[\[10\]](#ref-10). The capital investment and the depreciation risk have already been taken. What is missing in the 2027 budget is the far smaller cost of putting what has been built into production. The Ministry of Education and Research and the Ministry of Culture and Equality pay the capital cost. Then the Ministry of Digitalisation and Public Governance pays the rental cost all over again. That is not risk management; it is a lack of coordination.

**Joint purchasing power genuinely improves prices and terms.** This objection could make my analysis unfair to DFØ. When 318 public agencies pool their purchases into a single ten-billion-kroner CIPS agreement, they get lower unit prices [\[4\]](#ref-4). They also get stricter standard terms for privacy and security than any municipality could negotiate alone [\[4\]](#ref-4). Five million kroner for the Cloud Services Marketplace will almost certainly save the public sector many times that sum.

I do not doubt that DFØ will earn back those five million kroner. My point is what the discount does to the market structure. A volume discount from an oligopolist always serves two functions. It gives the buyer a short-term saving, and it raises the barrier to the buyer building a competitor. The better the discount DFØ negotiates on US cloud services in 2027, the worse a Norwegian or Nordic alternative will look in the next cost-benefit analysis. The discount is real, yet it simultaneously subsidises deeper lock-in.

**A public provider solves the 90 per cent of tasks that do not require frontier models.** This is an argument from my own camp, and it needs nuance. Advocates of a public AI and cloud service sometimes present it as if a state solution would make Norway self-sufficient. It would not. Even with Borealis in full operation on Olivia, healthcare, research, and defence will need specialised software. They will also need the largest global models for tasks that Norway's research community is too small to develop alone. A public provider does not eliminate dependency. It simply ensures that we are not dependent in *everything*. Everyday Norwegian public administration will not grind to a halt the day a foreign vendor changes its terms or its prices.

## What must be in the white paper if it is to mean anything

The government has given itself an opportunity to correct this course. The white paper on digital sovereignty and value creation was announced on 29 September 2026 [\[1\]](#ref-1). It is meant to examine digitalisation, artificial intelligence, data centres, and national security as a whole [\[1\]](#ref-1).

If the white paper is to be more than a summary noting that dependency is difficult, it must shift its gaze. It must move from the first class divide to the second and the third. That requires three concrete steps currently missing from the 2027 budget.

First, Digdir's 25-million-kroner shared solution must be given a national default endpoint at the bottom. When the shared gateway is built in 2027, Borealis and the open Norwegian models must be pre-selected as the default for unclassified casework [\[10\]](#ref-10). Operations can sit with Sigma2 or in the AI factory of Telenor, in which the Norwegian state holds a majority stake. External, token-priced models from foreign cloud vendors should be an opt-in choice when the task genuinely requires them. They should not be the default route for every piece of text in public administration.

Second, licensing policy for data centres must be tied to buy-back clauses and national preparedness capacity. NVE receives 20 million kroner to process applications for power and grid connections faster [\[3\]](#ref-3). In doing so, the directorate manages the scarcest input in the entire global AI economy: regulated, clean electrical power [\[1\]](#ref-1). That power should not be handed out for standard grid tariffs alone, only for the state to buy the compute back at full market price in the next round. Licensing terms for large data centres should include requirements for reserve capacity and priority access for critical public services in times of crisis. It is the exact same principle Norway used a century ago when it attached reversion rights (*heimfall*) and industrial power obligations to hydroelectric waterfall concessions.

Third, the state must stop keeping rent and ownership in two different accounting languages. Today, five million kroner to haggle over token prices looks like sound financial management. An allocation for domestic operating capacity, by contrast, looks like an expense. Yet rent that scales per token as public administration automates is not a saving. It is a permanent tax on public-sector productivity growth for the benefit of four or five foreign platform owners.

Access can be bought in a marketplace. Quality can be partly secured through multiple suppliers. Ownership of the returns on your own labour, however, is yours only to the extent that you own the tools you work with. As long as the 2.2 billion kroner in the state budget goes toward renting the floor and painting the doorframe, the landlord wins every time civil servants work faster.

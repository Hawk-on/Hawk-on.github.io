## The halt that was not about data centres

On 22 April 2026, Statnett, the state-owned operator of Norway's main power grid, put new reservations for large industrial consumption north of the Svartisen glacier on hold [\[1\]](#ref-1). No one got a new place in the queue, not even on special terms. Six months later, the Red Party (Raudt) cites the decision as a warning. The party writes that "similar situations" may arise in the rest of the country if data centres are allowed to continue as before [\[2\]](#ref-2).

It is worth reading what Statnett actually wrote. Its reasoning does not mention data centres. It mentions seafood, transport and the armed forces. Consumption in the region is set to rise by about 60 per cent in ten years, and the grid into the region cannot carry it [\[1\]](#ref-1).

So the halt in the north is not proof that data centres have taken the power. It proves something else: that the grid is already a scarce resource that has to be shared out. Whoever is first in the queue gets served. That is what the Red Party wants to change. Data centres are the largest group in the queue: 56 per cent of the projects waiting to reserve capacity with Statnett are data centres [\[3\]](#ref-3).

Figures from NVE, the Norwegian Water Resources and Energy Directorate, show how fast this has gone. In 2015, Norwegian data centres used hardly any power. In 2025, they used 3.3 TWh. Grid capacity reserved for new data centres rose from 169 MW in autumn 2020 to 4,217 MW in October 2026 [\[4\]](#ref-4). That is more than twenty times as much in six years.

Jobs have not kept pace. In April 2026, Statistics Norway (SSB) estimated that the industry employed 677 people in 2024. Of these, 344 were directly employed and 333 were hired in or attached to customers [\[5\]](#ref-5).

The Red Party's proposal is mainly about this: power, grid and jobs. But it also has another part, about who can demand access to the data stored in the halls. The two parts fit together less well than the proposal suggests. This article is about the gap between them.

## Ten years as a data centre nation

The data centres did not arrive by accident. They came because Norway invited them.

In 2016, large data centres were given a reduced electricity tax. According to the Red Party, ordinary customers paid 16.32 øre per kWh while data centres paid 0.48 øre. Two years later, the Conservative-led Solberg government launched the strategy "Norway as a data centre nation" and abolished property tax on data centres. In 2021, trade minister Linda Hofstad Helleland promised 11,000 jobs in the industry by 2025 [\[2\]](#ref-2). I have the figures for 2016 and 2021 only from the Red Party's proposal, not from primary sources.

The same year, on 19 July 2021, it was announced that the Israeli property group Azrieli Group would buy all of Green Mountain from the Norwegian investor Smedvig [\[6\]](#ref-6). Green Mountain is one of the best-known data centre companies in Norway. The purchase needed no national security clearance. Two years later, in 2023, Green Mountain's site at Hamar was brought under the Security Act [\[3\]](#ref-3). The control came after the sale, not before.

Then policy turned, slowly. The chronology:

- **1 January 2025:** A new Electronic Communications Act. Data centres above 500 kW must register, and security and preparedness requirements apply [\[7\]](#ref-7).
- **24 March 2025:** Digitalisation minister Karianne Tung rejects the Red Party's first proposal for a halt. Her reasoning is national: "For the government, it is important to secure a robust national infrastructure with fast and secure storage of national data and digital services" [\[8\]](#ref-8).
- **6 May 2025:** The proposal for a temporary ban falls. The Centre Party, the Socialist Left Party (SV) and the Red Party vote in favour [\[9\]](#ref-9).
- **27 June 2025:** A new data centre strategy. The government will "investigate the need for more data centres in Norway with Norwegian majority ownership" [\[7\]](#ref-7).
- **5 May 2026:** SV and the Red Party table a new moratorium proposal in a grid case. It also falls [\[10\]](#ref-10).
- **9 June 2026:** Parliament unanimously decides that the grid queue can be overridden for reasons of national security [\[11\]](#ref-11).
- **12 June 2026:** The US surveillance authority in FISA Section 702 expires. The surveillance continues [\[12\]](#ref-12).
- **29 June 2026:** A broad majority in Denmark, including the Red-Green Alliance (Enhedslisten), agrees an emergency plan for the grid. Data centres will as a rule go to the back of the queue [\[13\]](#ref-13).
- **1 October 2026:** The Danish parliament passes the law that enables the prioritisation, by 89 votes to 18 [\[14\]](#ref-14).
- **5 October 2026:** The Red Party tables Document 8:6 S, its third attempt [\[2\]](#ref-2).

Two things stand out. The first is that the government used a sovereignty argument against the halt in 2025. Storage of national data was the reason to keep the door open. The second is that the same summer, the government wrote that it would look at the need for more Norwegian-owned data centres. It shared the premise about ownership. It just did not want to stop the others while it thought about it.

## Two proposals in one document

Document 8:6 S has seven points [\[2\]](#ref-2). The first four are about power:

1. A halt on allocating power and grid capacity to new data centres until a new policy is in place. The halt is not to apply to data centres "that serve critical public functions and necessary public data storage".
2. A national licensing system for large power consumers, with social benefit as the criterion for grid connection. "National control over critical infrastructure" is among the considerations.
3. All projects of 25 MW or more are to be put before parliament, on the model of petroleum policy.
4. A new digitalisation strategy that also assesses national control over cloud services.

The last three are about jurisdiction:

5. The government is to assess whether data centres should come under the Security Act, including its rules on ownership control.
6. The government is to map which Norwegian data are under foreign jurisdiction through ownership of the infrastructure, including through the US CLOUD Act. The deadline is the end of 2027.
7. Data and systems essential to critical social functions are to be stored and processed under Norwegian jurisdiction, "or with suppliers that cannot be ordered to disclose data by a foreign state".

I should declare a tie. I am a member of SV, though not active in the party. SV stood with the Red Party behind the halt proposals in 2025 and in May 2026 [\[9\]](#ref-9)[\[10\]](#ref-10). I am assessing a policy my party has voted for.

What I want to show is that the two parts of the proposal act on different layers. The power part acts on the building: who gets power to the hall. The jurisdiction part acts on the service: who can demand the data in the hall. A halt on new halls does nothing about those already standing, and nothing about who runs the services inside them.

## Who owns the halls

In October 2026, the Norwegian Board of Technology (Teknologirådet), an independent advisory body to parliament, published a report on data centres in Norway. About 90 per cent of capacity is foreign-owned. About 8 per cent is owned by Norwegian private interests and about 2 per cent by municipalities and the state [\[3\]](#ref-3).

Ownership is also concentrated. Four facilities account for about two-thirds of connected capacity. Straitdeer/Bitdeer from Singapore has 26 per cent. Green Mountain, Israeli-owned, has 23 per cent. Google has 13 per cent. Polar/COWA has 6 per cent, with an ownership structure running through the UK, the Cayman Islands, Switzerland and the US [\[3\]](#ref-3).

Green Mountain is the best example of a brand name saying nothing about the owner. The company has a Norwegian name, Norwegian sites and Norwegian origins. The owner sits in Tel Aviv.

The government sees the same thing. The 2025 data centre strategy says data centres are where "we process and store some of our most sensitive and strategic data, making both ownership and location crucial to security interests" [\[7\]](#ref-7). The ministry has designated the ability to store and process data in Norway as a "fundamental national function" under the Security Act. The strategy also says some data centres are already subject to the Act and that more will be assessed [\[7\]](#ref-7).

So point 5 of the Red Party's proposal asks for something the government says it has already started. That is not an argument against the proposal. It is an argument for asking why it takes so long.

The Security Act has a tool that fits. Under section 1-3, second paragraph, a ministry can decide that chapter 10 applies to entities "of significant importance to fundamental national functions" [\[15\]](#ref-15). Chapter 10 governs ownership control. Anyone wanting to acquire a third or more of such an entity must notify the authorities. The King in Council, meaning the government formally, can block the acquisition if it poses a "not insignificant risk" to national security interests [\[15\]](#ref-15).

Parliament voted on 9 June 2023 to tighten this rule. The notification duty was to apply from 10 per cent, and again at 20 per cent, a third, 50 per cent, two-thirds and 90 per cent [\[16\]](#ref-16). According to Lovdata, the official legal database, the amendment has still not entered into force more than three years later [\[15\]](#ref-15). It is for the King in Council to decide when. The government has thus had a stricter tool available since 2023 without using it.

Green Mountain's Hamar site is now covered by this control. The Board of Technology writes that "the great majority of data centres in Norway" are not [\[3\]](#ref-3).

The Act controls acquisitions, not ownership that already exists. It could have stopped Azrieli in 2021 if the site had been under chapter 10 then. It was not. It cannot reverse the purchase now. That is the first limit on what ownership control can do.

The second limit matters more. Even if every hall were Norwegian-owned, that would not decide who can demand the data inside.

## The building is not the service

The CLOUD Act of 2018 is short. A US provider of electronic communication or remote computing services must disclose data within its "possession, custody, or control". The duty applies "regardless of whether such communication, record, or other information is located within or outside of the United States" [\[17\]](#ref-17).

The law does not mention buildings. It mentions control. The Board of Technology reads it the same way: the risk comes from US ownership "or use of US service providers" [\[3\]](#ref-3). This has two consequences that point in opposite directions.

First: a Norwegian-owned data centre where Microsoft or Amazon rents space and runs its cloud service is still fully under the CLOUD Act. Microsoft controls the data, not the landlord.

Second: a US-owned data centre where a Norwegian company rents space for its own servers does not give the landlord control over the data. A landlord renting out power, cooling and rack space does not hold the data in its "custody" in any ordinary sense. Here I move into interpretation. I have found no case law settling the question for pure colocation. But the wording points to whoever runs the service, not whoever owns the walls.

Microsoft has tried to meet the concern with promises. In April 2025, the company pledged to go to court if any government ordered it to suspend cloud operations in Europe [\[18\]](#ref-18). The promise covers suspending operations in Europe, not cutting off individual customers. And going to court is not the same as refusing.

The company has itself said how far its promises reach. In June 2025, the head of legal affairs at Microsoft France, Anton Carniaux, was questioned under oath by a French Senate inquiry. Could he guarantee that data on French citizens would never be handed to US authorities without French consent? "Non, je ne peux pas le garantir" (No, I cannot guarantee it) [\[19\]](#ref-19). He added that it had never happened.

How little promises and US legal changes count for became clear in June this year. The surveillance authority in FISA Section 702 expired on 12 June 2026 because Congress could not agree to renew it. Surveillance did not stop. The certifications approved by the FISA Court on 17 March 2026 remain valid until 17 March 2027, whatever happens to the statute. The Brennan Center, which wants stricter rules, writes that providers continue to cooperate, "as they must under the law" [\[12\]](#ref-12).

What does the data centre strategy say about this? It says the most critical services are to be delivered "from data centres in Norway or from data centres located with our allies" [\[7\]](#ref-7). The US is an ally. The strategy therefore treats US jurisdiction as part of the solution, not as a risk.

The term "CLOUD Act" does not appear in the English edition of the strategy. Nor did I find the word "jurisdiction". The strategy is about where the data are and who owns the building, not about which law applies to whoever runs the service.

That is why "Norwegian-owned data centres" is necessary but not sufficient. What decides is who runs the service. Point 7 of the Red Party's proposal is the only one that reaches this layer. It requires suppliers that cannot be ordered to disclose data by a foreign state. A halt on new halls does not.

## What sovereignty costs when it is called security

The strongest argument against national control has been price. When parliament considered a proposal for a state-run cloud service in May 2026, the reasoning was that a closed state cloud was the most expensive option in the government's concept study. I wrote about this in [The most expensive solution](/blog/den-dyraste-loysinga/) (in Norwegian). The external reviewers had a separate line for national autonomy in their assessment of effects that cannot be priced. The line was empty.

The data centre debate is conducted in the same currency. SSB measured value added per MWh. Data centres generated 829 kroner per MWh in 2024, against 912 kroner in traditional power-intensive industry [\[5\]](#ref-5). That is a useful number for comparing data centres with smelters. It says nothing about what it is worth that a foreign state cannot demand Norwegian health data.

It is hard to put a price on sovereignty and democratic self-government. But the state has recently shown that it does not always try. When parliament adopted a new section 3-8 of the Energy Act in June, the government gained authority to override the grid queue when "necessary to safeguard national security interests" [\[11\]](#ref-11).

The bill explains what the term covers. It is to be read as in the Security Act. The overarching interests are "the country's sovereignty, territorial integrity and democratic form of government and overarching security policy interests". Under letter d comes "economic stability and freedom of action" [\[20\]](#ref-20).

This was passed unanimously, without a cost-benefit analysis. No one asked what it costs to let a defence factory jump the queue. Sovereignty was the argument, and the argument was enough.

The same concept, sovereignty, was thus handled in two different ways in the same spring. When it was called digital infrastructure, it went through the accounts and lost. When it was called national security, it went straight through. Here I move into interpretation: the difference lies not in what is at stake, but in which ministry owns the case and which words it is written in.

The bill itself opens the door to data centres. The industry body Norsk Datasenterindustri asked in the consultation that "critical data centres" should be eligible for priority. The ministry replied that it did not want "either to include or exclude individual industries" [\[20\]](#ref-20). Both the Red Party and the industry thus ask for the same exemption for data centres serving critical functions. They disagree about the rest.

## Denmark sorted by purpose

The Red Party points to Denmark, and the comparison is relevant. On 29 June 2026, the Danish government and five other parties, including the Red-Green Alliance, agreed an emergency plan for the grid [\[13\]](#ref-13). The Danish parliament passed the law on 1 October by 89 votes to 18 [\[14\]](#ref-14). It was enacted as Act no. 866 of 7 October 2026.

The Act itself is short. It authorises the minister to set rules on how grid companies prioritise connection requests, and on refusing them [\[21\]](#ref-21). The word data centre does not appear in it. The sorting is in the political agreement, which divides requests into four categories [\[13\]](#ref-13):

1. Protected needs and critical functions: households, ordinary growth in consumption, defence, health and emergency services, "including data centres linked to critical functions".
2. Electrification, renewable generation, carbon capture, hydrogen and other green transition projects.
3. Energy storage and projects that support the power system.
4. "Certain large energy consumers". As a rule, this covers data centres that are not tied to a location and may strain the grid.

The agreement sorts by what the power will be used for. It does not mention who owns the project. Data centres serving critical functions go first. Other data centres go last.

The rhetoric was different. When the government presented the plan in June, climate and energy minister Samira Nawa said: "We will put the needs of society first, and then foreign data centres will have to queue at the back" [\[22\]](#ref-22). The minister spoke of foreign data centres. The agreement speaks of function. The difference is probably not accidental. A requirement based on nationality would be discrimination of the kind EU law strictly limits. A requirement based on function is easier to defend.

Norway is not in the EU but is bound by most of its single-market rules through the European Economic Area (EEA) Agreement. Bill Prop. 49 L shows how the Norwegian government reasons about the same problem. The ministry assumes that a priority decision may be a form of discrimination that must comply with the EU's third electricity market directive. It may also restrict the EEA's four freedoms. But "public security" is a legitimate ground under Articles 13, 33 and 39 of the EEA Agreement. If that fails, the ministry points to Article 123, which allows exceptions for essential national security interests [\[20\]](#ref-20).

Just fourteen months earlier, the energy minister had written the opposite about a similar proposal. Grid companies must be neutral, and "it is not for the grid companies to make political judgments about whether a planned consumption is desirable or not" [\[8\]](#ref-8). The neutral queue was a principle in March 2025. By June 2026 it was a principle with exceptions.

The lesson is not that anything goes. The lesson is that national security interests are the legal door, and that it is open. A requirement of Norwegian ownership would probably not get through it. A requirement that critical services be run by suppliers with no duty to disclose to foreign states has a much better chance. It is a requirement about function and jurisdiction, not nationality.

## The counterarguments in their strongest form

**A halt freezes ownership as it is.** This is the objection that could prove me most wrong. Statnett wrote of the northern halt that no one would lose reserved capacity [\[1\]](#ref-1). A national halt would probably rest on the same principle. The 4,217 MW already reserved for data centres would stay where they are [\[4\]](#ref-4). With 90 per cent foreign ownership, a halt protects those who came first and shuts out those who might have come later, Norwegian players included. The exemption for critical public functions builds nothing. It merely leaves a door open. I cannot refute this objection. What I can say is that a halt without a plan for who will build the critical capacity will entrench the ownership it is meant to challenge.

**The gap in value added is small.** SSB's figures show that in 2024 data centres were about ten per cent below traditional power-intensive industry in value added per MWh. In 2020 and 2021 they were above it [\[5\]](#ref-5). That weakens the picture of data centres as an industry that only takes. My answer is that value added per MWh was not the Red Party's only argument, and the figure says little about where the profit ends up. But the objection holds against those parts of the proposal that rest on data centres being poor industry.

**Ownership is not security.** The large cloud providers have more resources to defend against cyberattacks than any Norwegian player. A Norwegian operator may be easier to break into. That is true, but it concerns a different threat. The CLOUD Act is not a break-in. It is a lawful order, and it works however well the servers are protected.

**The EEA rules out nationality requirements.** Probably true, and that is why I think the requirement must be framed as function and jurisdiction. Prop. 49 L shows that the security exception exists. It does not show that it extends to all public data storage. That limit has not been tested. But the EU itself is moving the same way. In June 2026, the European Commission proposed a Cloud and AI Development Act. According to the Board of Technology, the proposal sorts cloud services into four levels, and public buyers must set requirements according to the level. At the strictest levels, the provider may not have a parent company outside the EU/EEA. The Board writes that a US-owned provider therefore cannot reach the highest level, "since the parent company will be subject to the CLOUD Act" [\[3\]](#ref-3). It is a proposal, not adopted law. But it shows that jurisdiction requirements in public procurement are not foreign to EU law.

**Foreign ownership is not foreign value creation.** The Board of Technology points out that a data centre with a foreign owner is still a Norwegian company. It pays tax in Norway just like a Norwegian-owned company [\[3\]](#ref-3). The 90 per cent figure therefore says little about where the money ends up. That is correct, and it is why this article does not rest on value creation. It rests on who can demand the data. The tax return does not answer that question.

**Investment moves to the neighbours.** A Norwegian halt could send projects to Sweden and Finland. Denmark has already put data centres at the back. If that happens, Norway loses the jobs, but not necessarily any sovereignty. Data in a US-run data centre in Sweden are under the same law as data in a US-run data centre in Norway.

## What a halt can buy

A halt can buy time. The question is what the time is used for.

If it is used for a new connection model, Norway gains control over its power. That is a worthy aim, and Denmark has shown it can be done. But it is not digital sovereignty. It is power policy.

If the time is also used for three other things, a halt can become more than power policy:

- **Ownership control over the right entities.** The ministry has authority under section 1-3, second paragraph, of the Security Act to apply chapter 10 to data centres of significant importance [\[15\]](#ref-15). The government said in June 2025 that this work had begun [\[7\]](#ref-7). It protects against the next Azrieli purchase, not against what has already happened.
- **Requirements on the operating layer, not just the building.** Point 7 of the Red Party's proposal is the only one that touches what actually decides jurisdiction. It can be done through public procurement: a requirement that critical data be run by suppliers that cannot be ordered to disclose data by a foreign state.
- **An honest mapping.** Point 6 asks the government to find out which Norwegian data are under foreign jurisdiction today. That a 2025 strategy on data centres and security does not mention the CLOUD Act suggests the mapping does not exist.

The government has announced a white paper on digital sovereignty and data centres. It follows a strategy that said ownership and location are crucial, and then said data centres with allies are good enough. The key test for the white paper is not whether it says something about power. It will. The test is whether it distinguishes between who owns the hall and who controls the service inside it.

The power flowing into the halls is Norwegian. The data stored there are not necessarily. A halt on new halls does not change that.
